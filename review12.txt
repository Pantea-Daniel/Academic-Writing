Review of “Experimental Comparison of Sorting Algorithms: Sequential and Parallel Implementations across Multiple Programming Language and Input Sizes”

The paper is in the scope of the assignment. It studies sorting algorithms, computational complexity, benchmarking methodology, and parallel implementations using POSIX threads. The topic is relevant and appropriate for a scientific paper in computer science.

This paper presents an experimental comparison of several classical sorting algorithms implemented in C, together with parallel versions of Merge Sort and Quick Sort. The algorithms are benchmarked on randomly generated integer arrays with input sizes ranging from 5,000 to 1,000,000 elements. The paper combines theoretical analysis with practical benchmarking and also discusses thread-level parallelism and timeout mechanisms for handling inefficient algorithms at large scale.

The presentation of the paper is clear and well structured. The work follows a strong academic organization, including introduction, formal algorithm descriptions, benchmarking setup, experiments, discussion, related work, conclusions, and reproducibility information. The paper is easy to follow and generally written in a more mature scientific style than many undergraduate projects.

The main results are important and meaningful for the level of the assignment. The experiments clearly demonstrate the practical limitations of O(n²) algorithms and confirm the expected scaling behaviour of O(n log n) methods. The inclusion of parallel variants is particularly valuable because it extends the comparison beyond basic sequential implementations. The benchmark results also show realistic observations regarding thread overhead and cache locality.

The results are reasonably non-trivial to achieve. Implementing sequential and parallel sorting algorithms in C using POSIX threads requires significant technical effort. The benchmarking framework, timeout mechanism, reproducibility setup, and cross-language repository organization also demonstrate careful project design. The paper goes beyond a simple classroom implementation and attempts to connect theoretical analysis with practical systems-level considerations.

The results seem to be original in the context of this assignment. The algorithms themselves are classical and well known, but the paper combines reproducible benchmarking, parallel implementations, timeout handling, and cross-language support into a coherent experimental study. The discussion of practical transition points between sequential and parallel efficiency is also useful.

The paper contains enough technical details to understand and mostly verify the correctness of the work. The theoretical sections are detailed, the benchmark methodology is explained carefully, and the complexity discussions are generally correct. The inclusion of references for both theoretical claims and implementation-related concepts strengthens the scientific quality of the paper. The reproducibility section and GitHub repository are also strong positive points.

From a technical perspective, the author appears to understand the subject well. I have partially checked the technical explanations and did not notice major conceptual errors. The discussion about cache locality, parallel overhead, asymptotic growth, and practical crossover points is convincing and consistent with standard algorithm theory. The benchmark tables and graphs on pages 7–9 also support the conclusions effectively.

The presentation quality is generally very good. The figures are relevant and readable, the tables are informative, and the bibliography is stronger than in most comparable student papers. The related work section is also properly integrated into the discussion instead of only listing references. The language is mostly formal and academic.

However, there are still several aspects that could be improved. Some sections are slightly denser than necessary, especially in the formal algorithm descriptions. A few explanations become mathematically heavy for the level of an undergraduate benchmark paper and could be simplified slightly for readability. There are also a few grammar and typography inconsistencies, such as repeated wording and small formatting issues. In addition, while the paper discusses cache effects theoretically, no actual hardware profiling or cache-miss measurements are included, so some claims remain inferential rather than experimentally verified.

For improvement, I would suggest including additional input distributions such as sorted, reverse sorted, or duplicate-heavy arrays directly in the experiments, rather than only proposing them in future work. Statistical analysis over multiple runs could also strengthen the reliability of the timing data. Another useful addition would be hardware profiling tools such as perf or VTune to experimentally validate the cache-locality discussion. Finally, some graphs could include logarithmic scaling or normalized speedup plots to make the parallel-performance analysis clearer.

Overall, this is a strong and technically mature university paper. The author demonstrates a solid understanding of sorting algorithms, asymptotic analysis, benchmarking methodology, and practical parallel programming. The experiments are meaningful, the implementation effort is significant, and the discussion successfully connects theoretical concepts with observed behaviour. While there are still some presentation and experimental limitations, the work is clearly above the average level for a student assignment.

Final recommendation: Accept.
